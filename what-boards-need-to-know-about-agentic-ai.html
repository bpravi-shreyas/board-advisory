What Boards Need to Know About Agentic AI — Before Their Management Teams Brief Them

By Raviprasad Bangalore | Senior Vice President & Head of Technology, Comviva (Tech Mahindra) | India's Top 10 AI Trailblazers — ET India

The Briefing Problem Nobody Is Talking About

There is a quiet conflict of interest sitting at the centre of most boardroom AI conversations right now.

Management teams are preparing Agentic AI investment proposals. They are also preparing the board briefings that will be used to approve those proposals. The same team asking for the budget is explaining why the budget is needed, what the risks are, and why the timeline is realistic.

This is not a new problem in governance. But with Agentic AI, the information asymmetry is sharper than it has been with any previous technology wave — including cloud, mobile, and even early GenAI. The reason is simple: most board members have not yet had the chance to build an independent mental model of what Agentic AI actually is, what it can and cannot do, and what the real governance questions are.

This article is written to close that gap — before your next briefing.

First, What Agentic AI Actually Is (In Plain Language)

Most boards have now heard about Generative AI. ChatGPT made that unavoidable. But Agentic AI is a meaningful step beyond — and the distinction matters enormously from a governance perspective.

Generative AI responds. You give it a prompt, it gives you an output. A human reviews it, decides what to do with it, and acts. The human remains in the loop at every decision point.

Agentic AI acts. You give it a goal. It breaks that goal into tasks, decides how to execute each task, calls on tools and data sources, makes decisions along the way, and completes the objective — often without a human reviewing each step.

Think of it this way: Generative AI is a very capable assistant who answers your questions. Agentic AI is an autonomous colleague who takes on a project, makes judgment calls, and delivers results.

That shift — from responding to acting — is what changes the governance equation entirely.

Why This Is a Board-Level Issue, Not Just a Technology Issue

When a system can act autonomously — accessing data, triggering workflows, making decisions, interacting with customers or suppliers — the questions that matter are no longer just technical. They are governance questions.

Who authorised the agent to act on behalf of the organisation? Most Agentic AI deployments today are being approved at the technology or business unit level. The board is rarely involved in defining the boundaries of what an agent is permitted to do. That is a governance gap — and in regulated industries, it is potentially a liability gap.

What happens when an agent makes a wrong decision at scale? A human making a wrong decision affects one outcome. An agent making a wrong decision can affect thousands of outcomes before anyone notices. The speed and scale of Agentic AI amplifies both the upside and the downside. Boards need to ask: what is our error detection and containment framework?

Who is accountable when an agent causes harm? This is the question that regulators in the EU, UK, and increasingly India are beginning to formalise. The EU AI Act, which came into force in 2024, places explicit obligations on organisations deploying high-risk AI systems. Boards that have not mapped their Agentic AI deployments against regulatory risk frameworks are operating with incomplete oversight.

Is the data the agent is using clean, consented, and governed? Agents are only as good as the data they act on. If the underlying data has quality issues, bias, or consent gaps, the agent will amplify those problems — not correct them. Data governance is now inseparable from AI governance.

The Five Questions Every Board Member Should Ask

These are not technical questions. They are governance questions — and any management team presenting an Agentic AI proposal should be able to answer them clearly. If they cannot, that is important information.

1. What decisions is this agent authorised to make autonomously — and what decisions require human review?

This is the boundary question. Every Agentic AI deployment should have a clearly defined decision boundary — what the agent can do without human approval, and what it cannot. If management cannot articulate this boundary precisely, the governance framework is not ready.

2. How do we detect when an agent is making errors — and how quickly can we intervene?

Agentic AI systems can fail in ways that are not immediately visible. Unlike a software bug that crashes a system, an agent making poor decisions may continue operating normally while producing wrong outcomes. Ask for the monitoring, alerting, and intervention framework — not just the deployment plan.

3. What is our regulatory exposure?

Depending on the sector and the use case, Agentic AI deployments may fall under existing regulations (data protection, financial services conduct, healthcare privacy) or emerging AI-specific regulations. The board should know which regulatory frameworks apply and whether legal and compliance have reviewed the deployment.

4. What is the worst-case scenario — and have we stress-tested it?

Every technology investment proposal presents the upside case. Ask for the downside case. What happens if the agent acts on corrupted data? What happens if it is manipulated by a bad actor? What happens if it makes a decision that harms a customer? Has the organisation run a structured risk scenario exercise?

5. How are we measuring whether this is actually working?

Agentic AI investments are often justified with efficiency projections — cost savings, productivity gains, turnaround-time reductions. Ask how these will be measured, over what timeframe, and what the decision criteria are for scaling, pausing, or stopping the deployment. Vague success metrics are a red flag.

What Good Agentic AI Governance Looks Like

Boards that are ahead of this curve are doing several things that others are not yet doing.

They have an AI governance framework — not just an AI strategy. Strategy tells you where you are going. Governance tells you how decisions will be made, who is accountable, and what the guardrails are. Many organisations have the former without the latter. A board that only reviews AI strategy without reviewing AI governance is only doing half its job.

They have separated the investment decision from the governance review. The question of whether to invest in Agentic AI and the question of how that investment will be governed are two different conversations. Boards that conflate them — approving the investment and the governance framework in the same meeting — are moving too fast.

They have at least one director who can interrogate the technology independently. This does not mean a director who can write code. It means a director who understands enough about how Agentic AI systems work — architecturally, operationally, and commercially — to ask questions that management cannot easily deflect with jargon. The value of that director in a board conversation is disproportionate to their number.

They are asking about the human-in-the-loop design — not just the automation rate. The most sophisticated boards are not asking "how much can we automate?" They are asking "where must a human remain in the loop, and how have we designed for that?" That is a fundamentally different question — and it reflects a mature understanding of where Agentic AI governance actually lives.

The Asymmetry You Need to Correct

Here is the honest reality of where most boards sit today.

Management teams working on Agentic AI have been living with this technology for 12–24 months. They have built systems, run pilots, seen failures, iterated, and developed intuitions about what works and what does not. They have a rich, textured understanding of the technology.

Most board members are receiving their first structured briefing on Agentic AI from that same management team — in a 20-minute slot on a board agenda, with a deck that has been designed to build confidence, not surface doubt.

That asymmetry is not anyone's fault. But it is a governance problem. And the way to correct it is not to become a technologist. It is to develop an independent mental model — one that gives you the right questions, the right instincts, and the confidence to push back when something does not add up.

The five questions above are a starting point. The governance framework questions are the next layer. And the willingness to say "we are not ready to approve this until the governance framework is as robust as the investment case" — that is the board's most important contribution to getting Agentic AI right.

A Final Thought

Every major technology wave of the last three decades has produced the same pattern: early adopters move fast, governance catches up slowly, and the organisations that get hurt are the ones where the board was the last to understand what was actually happening.

Agentic AI is moving faster than cloud, faster than mobile, and faster than early GenAI. The window for boards to develop independent oversight capability — before the decisions that matter have already been made — is shorter than it has ever been.

The briefing from your management team is coming. The question is whether you are ready to receive it as an informed, independent governor — or as an audience.
